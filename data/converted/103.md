The Mechitza:
Symbol of the Orthodox Synagogue
In modern times, the Reform movement attempted to weaken
and altogether abrogate the mechitza. the Reform Congregation
of Berlin was the first to remove its mechitza in 5605 (1845). the
men and women sat separately on the same floor (but without a
mechitza). the dishonour of being first to introduce entirely mixed
seating belongs to reform Rabbi Isaac Mayer Wise at Congregation
anshe Emeth in albany, NY in 5611 (1851). In response to these and
other changes, the orthodox rabbis of Hungary declared in the year
5626 (1866), “It is forbidden to construct the mechitza between men
and women in a manner where the men can view the women, rather
it should be made as in time immemorial. One should not enter
such a synagogue.”16
From the 1950s, the mechitza became the most obvious distin-
guishing mark between orthodox shuls and conservative temples. In
many shuls, disputes reached as far as the secular courts them-
selves. the most famous amongst them was led by Mr. Baruch Litvin
of Congregation Beth tephilath Moses in Mt. Clemens, Michigan.
after four years of legal battles, it was decided in the Supreme Court
of Michigan that mixed seating in that shul was forbidden.
an article published in Jewish telegraphic agency on June 15,
1959 states, “Mr. Litvin, who claims to have spent $12,000 in battling
the suit for four years, stated that the court’s decision was not a
personal triumph, but a victory for Orthodox Judaism.”17
In 1959, Mr. Litvin published a sefer in both Hebrew and English
called the Sanctity of the Synagogue, in which he presents all the
halachic sources on the matter. On the Hebrew side (page 90), a copy
of a letter from the Rebbe to him is printed. In it, the Rebbe congrat-
ulates him for his victory in court. Letters printed in Igros Kodesh18
demonstrate that Mr. Litvin was in frequent contact with the Rebbe
from at least 5709 (1949), and that the Rebbe also encouraged him
and advised him throughout the duration of the court case.

Q1: How high does the mechitza have to be?
A: As quoted above from the Rebbe, the mechitza should be at least
six feet high.
This is because, according to most poskim, the goal of the mechitza is to block the
men from viewing the women. As such, it must be above the height of women.
In Shaylos uTeshuvos Knei Bosem )ופוסב ט 'יס ב"ח(, HaRav Meir Brandsdorfer notes
that the mechitza must be tall enough that even the men standing on the bimah
and before the Aron HaKodesh (at the time the Torah is being taken out) should be
unable to see the women. If so, in some cases the mechitza must be significantly
higher than the height of men standing on the floor of the shul.
His words are quite logical. Do we find anywhere that laining (in middle of daven-
ing) is exempted from mechitza? Considering the platform as a halachically sepa-
rate domain is a non-starter as the purpose of the mechitza is also to exclude the
possibility of seeing the women.
Additionaly, the Rebbe’s words may allude to this approach. As quoted above, the
Rebbe writes “the mechitza should be at least six feet high,” which apparently means
that sometimes even six feet is not really tall enough. Now, let’s take a look at the num-
bers: An average women is 5’4” tall. Only an extremely tall woman would be visible over
a six-foot mechitza, yet the Rebbe preferred for the mechitza to be above six feet tall.
It is sensible to say that the Rebbe wished that the mechitza block the view even while
