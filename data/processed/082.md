# [Misc > For Writer > _5 Chanukah > General] Strategy After Denial: The One-on-One Meeting Approach

## Forum Success Story: From Denial to Approval

**Situation:** Township denied permission for menorah on town property

**Solution:** Strategic one-on-one meeting with council member

**Result:** Approval granted

---

## The Problem

### Initial Denial

> "The local township Denied permission to display a menorah on town property."

**Common Scenario:**
- Formal request submitted
- Township said no
- Dead end through official channels
- Need new approach

---

## The Solution: The Meeting Strategy

### Step 1: Identify the Right Person

**Who to Call:**
> "I called one of the council members and asked to meet with him."

**Not:**
- ❌ The whole council
- ❌ The mayor (initially)
- ❌ Staff/bureaucrats
- ❌ Multiple officials at once

**But:**
- ✅ ONE council member
- ✅ Someone sympathetic or neutral
- ✅ Person with influence
- ✅ Individual conversation

**Why One-on-One Works:**
- No performative opposition
- Casual, honest discussion
- Can build relationship
- They can become your champion
- Less formal, more effective

---

### Step 2: Request a Meeting

**Approach:**
- Call directly
- "I'd like to meet with you to discuss..."
- Brief, respectful request
- Convenient for them
- One meeting, specific topic

**Setting:**
- Private office
- Coffee shop
- Informal if possible
- Face-to-face (not phone/email)
- No audience

---

## The Meeting: What to Say

### Frame 1: Diversity and Community

**Lead With:**
> "At the meeting I suggested the menorah lighting as a way to bring diversity to the community."

**Why This Works:**
- Positive framing
- Benefits the community
- Not just for Jews
- Official looks good
- Win-win proposition

**Talking Points:**
- "Celebrates our community's diversity"
- "Makes Jewish residents feel included"
- "Shows town welcomes all faiths"
- "Other towns are doing this"
- "Brings community together"

---

### Frame 2: Context Matters

**The Subtle Suggestion:**
> "You can even say it will be next to the tree (you understand)"

**The Wink:**
- Everyone knows the Supreme Court case
- Menorah next to tree = constitutional
- You're making it easy for them
- Giving them the solution
- "You understand" = we both know the law

**How to Say It:**
- "We can place it next to the community Christmas tree"
- "Like in [city name] - no problems there"
- "The Supreme Court specifically approved this setup"
- "Makes it clearly constitutional"

---

### Frame 3: Photo Opportunity

**The Sweetener:**
> "It can also be a photo opportunity for the city official."

**Politicians Love:**
- Positive press
- Community engagement
- Diverse constituency appeal
- Holiday season goodwill
- Photo with constituents

**Offer:**
- "Mayor can light the menorah"
- "Great photo op for local paper"
- "Shows commitment to all residents"
- "Invite entire city council"
- "Positive press for the city"

---

### Frame 4: Broad Appeal

**Supporting Materials:**
> "I also brought along articles that I gathered where people (especially not Jewish) saying how wonderful it is to have the lighting."

**What to Bring:**

**Articles Showing:**
- Gentile support for menorahs
- Positive coverage from other cities
- Community testimonials
- "Even non-Jews love this"
- No controversy, just positive

**Why This Matters:**
- Official fears controversy
- Show it's uncontroversial
- Broad community support
- Not just Jewish issue
- Safe decision

---

### Frame 5: Addressing Alienation

**The Emotional Appeal:**
> "It is also a chance for the city officials to say a few words and make the Jewish constituency feel more welcome at a time when they are alienated."

**Key Message:**
- Jewish residents feel left out
- Especially during Christmas season
- City can fix this easily
- Make everyone feel included
- Officials can speak about inclusion

**How to Present:**
- "Jewish families appreciate being acknowledged"
- "During the Christmas season, they feel marginalized"
- "This simple gesture means so much"
- "You'd be surprised how much goodwill this generates"
- "Brings out positive feelings, not controversy"

---

## The Legal Framework: Second Meeting

### When to Introduce

**Note in Original:**
> "In your meeting you might also tell them about the Supreme Court ruling."

**Timing:**
- After establishing relationship
- After positive framing
- As supporting evidence
- Not as threat
- More "by the way, it's also legal"

---

### Two Key Points

**Point 1: It's a Big Organization**
> "You are not just one small rabbi in a small town, but represent a worldwide organization"

**Why This Matters:**
- Shows staying power
- Not going away
- Resources available
- Can escalate if needed
- But not threatening—just fact

**How to Say:**
- "Chabad is a worldwide organization"
- "We're in [X] cities nationwide"
- "This is routine for us"
- "We have resources and experience"
- "But we prefer working together"

---

**Point 2: Their Action Was Illegal**
> "What they did was illegal"

**Clarification:**
- Denying permission violates free speech
- Supreme Court precedent
- Other towns learned this
- Can demonstrate with cases
- Better to fix now than face lawsuit

**How to Say:**
- "The denial raises constitutional concerns"
- "Supreme Court has ruled on this"
- "Other towns have had to reverse similar denials"
- "Much easier to approve now"
- "Saves everyone time and trouble"

---

## The Approach: Gevurah and Chesed

### The Philosophy

**The Formula:**
> "Be careful, mix the Gevurah and Chessed, the approach should be the first way. Use the Gevruah on the second round."

**Translation:**
- **Chesed (kindness) first:** Positive, cooperative, win-win
- **Gevurah (strength) second:** Legal rights, organization backing

---

### First Meeting: Chesed

**Lead With:**
- ✅ Benefits to community
- ✅ Diversity celebration
- ✅ Photo opportunity
- ✅ Positive press
- ✅ Jewish residents' feelings
- ✅ Gentile support too
- ✅ How to make it work

**Tone:**
- Warm
- Collaborative
- Solution-oriented
- Helpful
- "We can make this great for everyone"

---

### Second Meeting (If Needed): Gevurah

**Introduce:**
- Legal framework
- Supreme Court cases
- Organization backing
- "What they did was illegal"
- Other towns' experience

**Tone:**
- Still respectful
- But firmer
- "This needs to be resolved"
- "We have options"
- "Prefer cooperation but prepared to escalate"

**Not Threatening:**
- Don't say "we'll sue"
- Just factual
- "The law is clear"
- "This is our right"
- "Let's find a solution"

---

## Who Should Do the Talking?

### Consider a Champion

**Alternative Approach:**
> "If you know somebody that has lots of clout with the city officials ask them to do the talking."

**Who Has Clout:**
- Major donor to city officials
- Prominent business person
- Well-connected community member
- Person who helped official get elected
- Someone they can't easily refuse

**Advantages:**
- More credibility with officials
- Less "religious agenda" perception
- Peer-to-peer conversation
- Harder to say no
- Can be more direct

**Your Role:**
- Prep them with information
- Provide materials
- Available for questions
- But let them lead
- Thank them afterwards

---

## Why This Strategy Works

### Psychology of One-on-One

**Group Meeting:**
- Officials posture
- Play to audience
- Harder to change minds
- Public positions taken
- Face-saving becomes issue

**One-on-One:**
- Honest conversation
- Can explore options
- No audience to perform for
- Relationship building
- Can change mind without embarrassment

---

### The Escalation Ladder

**Step 1: Request (denied)**
- Official channels
- Formal application
- Got "no"

**Step 2: Meeting (this strategy)**
- Personal conversation
- Reframe the issue
- Build relationship
- Show how to say yes

**Step 3: Legal (if needed)**
- Only after personal approach fails
- But foundation laid
- They know your position
- Less surprise

---

### The Champion Effect

**When Someone with Clout Asks:**
- Official owes them favors
- Peer pressure
- "Help me help my friend"
- Political capital
- Much harder to refuse

**Example:**
> Council Member thinks: "This donor helped my campaign. If I can do this small favor, I should. Plus, it sounds legal and uncontroversial when he explains it."

---

## Practical Implementation

### Before the Meeting

**Research:**
- Which council member is most sympathetic?
- Who has Jewish constituents?
- Who is open-minded?
- Who might have personal connection?
- Check voting records

**Prepare Materials:**
- Positive articles
- Photos from other cities
- Supreme Court excerpt
- One-page summary
- Contact information

**Think Through:**
- What's in it for them?
- What are their concerns?
- How can you make it easy?
- What's your ask exactly?
- Alternative locations if needed?

---

### During the Meeting

**Listen First:**
- What are their concerns?
- Why was it denied?
- What would make them comfortable?
- Who opposed it?
- What's the real issue?

**Address Concerns:**
- Match solution to their specific worry
- Don't give generic pitch
- Respond to what they say
- Show you understand
- Make it about solving their problem

**End With Clear Next Steps:**
- "What would you need to support this?"
- "Can you bring this to the council?"
- "Would you be willing to light it?"
- "When can we follow up?"
- "What's the process from here?"

---

### After the Meeting

**Follow Up:**
- Thank you note/email
- Provide any promised materials
- Check in before next council meeting
- Update on any developments
- Keep them informed

**If Supportive:**
- Make them your champion
- Keep them looking good
- Provide talking points for council
- Offer to meet with others
- Show gratitude

## When to Use This Strategy

### Best For:

**Small to Medium Communities:**
- Where one-on-one relationships matter
- Personal connections count
- Officials accessible
- Less bureaucratic

**After Initial Denial:**
- Official channels didn't work
- Need different approach
- Before resorting to legal action
- Building relationship for future

**When You Have Time:**
- Not emergency
- Can develop relationship
- Multiple meetings possible
- Strategic long-term approach

---

### Maybe Not For:

**Large Cities:**
- Too bureaucratic
- One council member may not matter
- Process more formal
- Harder to access officials

**Hostile Environment:**
- Organized opposition
- Council united against you
- Legal action may be only option
- But still worth trying first

**Emergency Timeline:**
- Chanukah in one week
- No time for relationship building
- May need immediate legal action
- Though one urgent meeting might still work

## Key Takeaways

### The Meeting Strategy

**1. One-on-One Is Powerful**
- More effective than group meetings
- Builds relationships
- Allows honest conversation
- Easier to change minds

**2. Frame Positively First**
- Diversity
- Community benefit
- Photo opportunity
- Gentile support
- Makes them look good

**3. Chesed Before Gevurah**
- Start collaborative
- Make it easy to say yes
- Legal arguments second
- Build relationship first

**4. Consider a Champion**
- Someone with clout
- Makes your case
- Peer-to-peer more effective
- You support behind scenes

**5. Prepare Thoroughly**
- Know your council member
- Bring supporting materials
- Anticipate concerns
- Have solutions ready

---

### The Sequence

1. ✅ Get initial denial
2. ✅ Don't give up
3. ✅ Identify sympathetic council member
4. ✅ Request one-on-one meeting
5. ✅ Frame positively (diversity, photo op, articles)
6. ✅ Build relationship
7. ✅ If needed, second meeting with legal framework
8. ✅ Official becomes your champion
9. ✅ Get approval

---

**Bottom Line:**
After township denial, this Shaliach succeeded by requesting one-on-one meeting with a council member—the key strategy is personal relationship over formal process. In the meeting, lead with positive framing: menorah as "diversity," photo opportunity for official, show articles of gentile support, address alienation of Jewish residents. Bring supporting materials but keep it conversational. The formula: "Mix Gevurah and Chesed" - start with kindness (benefits, cooperation, solutions) and only introduce legal framework (Supreme Court, worldwide organization, "what they did was illegal") in second meeting if needed. Consider having someone with local clout do the talking instead of you—officials respond better to peer pressure than rabbi requests. The psychology: group meetings breed posturing, but one-on-one allows honest conversation, relationship building, and face-saving path to "yes." This strategy works best in small-medium communities where personal relationships matter, after initial formal denial but before legal action, when you have time to develop the relationship strategically. Remember: make it easy for them to look good while doing the right thing.
