# [Misc > For Writer > _5 Chanukah > General] Supreme Court Case: Public Menorah Display Constitutional

## U.S. Supreme Court Decision Establishing Legal Precedent

**Case:** County of Allegheny v. American Civil Liberties Union, Greater Pittsburgh Chapter

**Citation:** 492 U.S. 573 (1989)

**Decided:** July 3, 1989

**Significance:** Landmark ruling permitting public menorah displays

---

## Case Summary

### The Displays in Question

**Location:** Downtown Pittsburgh public property

**Display #1: Creche**
- Placed on Grand Staircase of Allegheny County Courthouse
- Donated by Holy Name Society (Roman Catholic group)
- Banner proclaimed "Gloria in Excelsis Deo" ("Glory to God in the Highest")
- Most prominent, beautiful, public part of courthouse

**Display #2: Chanukah Menorah**
- 18-foot Chanukah menorah
- Placed outside City-County Building
- Next to city's 45-foot decorated Christmas tree
- Sign at tree's foot: mayor's "salute to liberty"
- Menorah owned by Chabad
- Stored, erected, and removed annually by city

---

### The Challenge

**Plaintiffs:** ACLU Greater Pittsburgh Chapter + seven local residents

**Claim:** Both displays violated Establishment Clause of First Amendment

**District Court:** Denied relief (relied on Lynch v. Donnelly)

**Court of Appeals:** Reversed - both displays unconstitutional endorsement of religion

**Supreme Court:** SPLIT DECISION
- **Creche:** Unconstitutional (affirmed)
- **Menorah:** Constitutional (reversed)

---

## The Supreme Court's Ruling

### On the Creche: UNCONSTITUTIONAL

**Majority Opinion (Justice Blackmun):**

> "When viewed in its overall context, the creche display violates the Establishment Clause. The creche angel's words endorse a patently Christian message: Glory to God for the birth of Jesus Christ."

**Key Distinctions from Lynch v. Donnelly:**
- Nothing in setting detracts from religious message
- No secular symbols surrounding it
- Placed in most prominent government location
- Government appearing to praise God for Jesus's birth

**Why This Matters:**
- Government may acknowledge Christmas as cultural phenomenon
- BUT cannot observe it as Christian holy day
- Cannot suggest people should praise God for birth of Jesus

---

### On the Menorah: CONSTITUTIONAL

**Majority Opinion (Justice Blackmun):**

> "The menorah display does not have the prohibited effect of endorsing religion, given its 'particular physical setting.'"

**Why It's Permitted:**

**1. Combined Display Context**
- Christmas tree (secular symbol)
- Menorah (religious symbol)
- "Salute to Liberty" sign
- Together = recognition of cultural diversity, not endorsement

**2. Christmas Tree as Predominant Element**
- 45 feet vs. 18 feet
- Central position
- Clearly the main feature
- Menorah is secondary

**3. Message Conveyed**
- Both holidays part of same winter season
- Both have attained secular status in our society
- Recognition that Christmas not only way to celebrate season
- Celebrates pluralism and freedom

**4. No Secular Alternative**
- Unlike creche (could use Santa, reindeer, etc.)
- Menorah is THE symbol of Chanukah
- Absence of alternative negates inference of endorsement

**5. Mayor's Sign**
- "Salute to liberty"
- Confirms recognition of cultural diversity
- Not sponsorship of religious beliefs
- Emphasizes freedom theme

---

## Justice O'Connor's Concurring Opinion

### Her Framework (The "Endorsement Test")

**Question:** Would a reasonable observer view the display as government endorsement or disapproval of religion?

**Her Analysis:**
- Christmas tree = secular symbol (whatever its origins)
- Menorah = religious symbol (Chanukah primarily religious holiday)
- **Together** = message of pluralism and freedom
- Context prevents reasonable interpretation as endorsement

**Key Quote:**
> "By including the menorah with the tree, and with the sign saluting liberty, the city conveyed a message of pluralism and freedom of belief during the holiday season."

---

## Justice Kennedy's Dissent (Would Permit Both)

### His Position

**Both displays should be constitutional.**

**His Reasoning:**

**1. Government Accommodation of Religion**
- Policies of accommodation are accepted part of heritage
- Establishment Clause permits government latitude
- Recognizing central role of religion in society

**2. Not Proselytization**
- No coercion to support or participate
- No direct benefits establishing state religion
- No effort to convert anyone
- Purely passive symbols

**3. Historical Practice**
- Many unchallenged religious acknowledgments throughout history
- Legislative chaplains (Marsh v. Chambers)
- "In God We Trust" on currency
- Thanksgiving proclamations

**4. The Alternative Is Worse**
- Recognizing only secular = "callous indifference"
- Would require government to exclude religious
- Borders on hostility to religion

**Joined by:** Chief Justice Rehnquist, Justice White, Justice Scalia

---

## Key Legal Principles Established

### The Lemon Test

**From Lemon v. Kurtzman (1971):**

Government practice touching religion must:
1. Have secular purpose
2. Not advance or inhibit religion (primary effect)
3. Not create excessive entanglement with religion

**This case refined #2:** "Advance" means endorsement, favoritism, preference, or promotion

---

### The Endorsement Test

**From Justice O'Connor's Framework:**

> Government violates Establishment Clause when it makes "adherence to a religion relevant in any way to a person's standing in the political community."

**Application:**
- Would reasonable observer see endorsement?
- Consider overall context
- Look at physical setting
- Evaluate message conveyed
- Account for secular elements

---

### Context Matters Enormously

**Same Symbol, Different Results:**

**Creche Alone in Courthouse:**
- Unconstitutional endorsement
- Government taking position
- Religious message primary

**Menorah with Tree and Liberty Sign:**
- Constitutional acknowledgment
- Cultural diversity message
- Secular context predominates

## Practical Implications for Shluchim

### What This Ruling Permits

**Public Menorah Displays ARE ALLOWED When:**

✅ Displayed with secular holiday symbols
- ✅ Christmas tree nearby
- ✅ "Season's greetings" type signage
- ✅ Lights, decorations, etc.

✅ Conveys message of pluralism/diversity
- Not "Judaism is true"
- But "We celebrate religious freedom"
- Multiple traditions recognized

✅ No coercion or proselytization
- Passive display
- No religious services at location
- No pressure to participate

✅ Liberty/freedom theme emphasized
- Signs about freedom
- References to religious liberty
- American values language

---

### What To Avoid

**Risk Factors That Could Make Display Unconstitutional:**

❌ Menorah alone with no secular context
❌ Religious services held at display
❌ Government officials leading prayers
❌ Display in most sacred government space
❌ Religious messaging without secular balance
❌ Excluding other faiths from participation

## Historical and Religious Context from Opinion

### Chanukah Background (As Described by Court)

**The Historical Event:**
- 164 BCE (165 BC)
- Maccabees recaptured Temple of Jerusalem
- From Seleucid Empire (Greek-influenced)
- Political rebellion with religious dimensions
- Rededicated Temple on 25th of Kislev

**The Miracle:**
- Temple menorah needed to burn continuously
- Only enough oil for one day
- Oil miraculously lasted eight days
- Time needed to obtain more oil

**The Ritual:**
- Mitzvah (religious commandment)
- Place lamp with eight lights outside home or in window
- Celebrate and publicly proclaim miracle
- Recite blessings before lighting
- Each night of eight-day holiday

---

### Cultural Dimensions the Court Recognized

**Not Purely Religious:**
1. **National/Political Dimension**
   - Story of national heroism
   - Military victory
   - Political independence
   - Emphasized especially in Israel

2. **Cultural/Ethnic Dimension**
   - Some nonreligious Jews celebrate
   - Expression of ethnic identity
   - Cultural or national event
   - Like some Americans celebrate Christmas secularly

3. **Winter Holiday Dimension**
   - Associated with winter solstice in ancient times
   - Proximity to Christmas
   - Part of "holiday season" in America
   - Giving of Chanukah gelt enhanced by Christmas timing

4. **American Context**
   - Greater social prominence than religious significance would suggest
   - Lower in hierarchy of Jewish holidays religiously
   - But widely observed in America
   - Heightened cultural/secular status

**Court's Understanding:**
> "Chanukah, like Christmas, is a cultural event as well as a religious holiday."

## Technical Details About Menorah

### From the Court's Description

**Temple Menorah:**
- Seven branches
- Central candlestick + six branches
- Tree-and-branch design
- Burned continuously
- Sanctified object

**Chanukah Menorah:**
- Eight branches (one per day)
- Plus ninth for shamash (helper candle)
- Tree-and-branch design evokes Temple
- Not a sanctified object
- Need not be treated with special care
- No specific rule on shape or substance in Jewish law

**Traditional Placement:**
- Just outside entrance to home
- Or in front window
- Where practicality/safety requires: inside
- Public proclamation of miracle

**Court's Note:**
> "Lighting the menorah is the primary tradition associated with Chanukah."

**On Context:**
> "The placement of the menorah beside [the Christmas tree] is readily understood as simply a recognition that Christmas is not the only traditional way of celebrating the season."

**On Liberty Theme:**

---

### From Justice O'Connor's Concurrence

**On Message Conveyed:**
> "The city conveyed a message of pluralism and freedom of belief during the holiday season."

**On Reasonable Observer:**
> "Could not be interpreted by a reasonable observer as an endorsement of Judaism or Christianity or disapproval of alternative beliefs."

---

### From Justice Kennedy's Opinion

**On Accommodation:**
> "Government policies of accommodation, acknowledgement, and support for religion are an accepted part of our political and cultural heritage."

> "The creche and menorah are purely passive symbols of religious holidays."

## Bottom Line

### The Holding

**Public menorah displays on government property ARE CONSTITUTIONAL when:**
1. Displayed alongside secular holiday symbols (especially Christmas tree)
2. Secular symbols are predominant
3. Signage emphasizes liberty, freedom, diversity
4. Overall message is pluralism, not religious endorsement
5. No coercion or religious services

**This is settled Supreme Court precedent since 1989.**

**Bottom Line:**
This landmark 1989 Supreme Court decision explicitly permits public menorah displays when contextualized with secular holiday symbols and liberty/pluralism messaging. The 18-foot Chabad menorah in Pittsburgh, displayed next to a 45-foot Christmas tree and "Salute to Liberty" sign, was held constitutional because the overall message conveyed cultural diversity rather than religious endorsement. For Shluchim, this provides firm legal ground for requesting public menorah displays: frame the request around freedom, pluralism, and cultural celebration; include secular context; emphasize the liberty theme of Chanukah; and cite this precedent directly. The Court's reasoning - that the combined display "simply recognizes that both Christmas and Chanukah are part of the same winter-holiday season" and "conveys a message of pluralism and freedom" - gives both legal and rhetorical framework for successful public menorah campaigns. This is not asking for a favor; it's an established constitutional right affirmed by the nation's highest court.
