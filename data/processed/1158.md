## English Translation

**Opening Question**:
Why is this night, so very different, from all other nights, from all other nights?

**First Question - Matzah**:
On all other nights, we could eat, chametz or matza, chametz or matza,
But on this night, but on this night, only matza,

**Second Question - Vegetables/Maror**:

**Third Question - Dipping**:

**Fourth Question - Reclining**:

## The Four Questions Explained

### 1. Matzah Question
**Question**: Why do we eat only matzah on this night?
**Answer**: Matzah reminds us of the haste with which the Jewish people left Egypt - they didn't have time for their dough to rise.

### 2. Maror (Bitter Herbs) Question
**Question**: Why do we eat bitter herbs on this night?
**Answer**: The bitter herbs remind us of the bitterness of slavery that our ancestors experienced in Egypt.

### 3. Dipping Question
**Question**: Why do we dip twice on this night?
**Answer**: We dip twice - first the karpas (vegetable) in salt water symbolizing the tears shed during slavery, and second the maror in charoset symbolizing the mortar and bricks of hard labor.

### 4. Reclining Question
**Question**: Why do we recline while eating on this night?
**Answer**: Reclining was the manner of free people in ancient times. We recline to demonstrate that we are free people, no longer slaves.

## For Shluchim: Educational and Practical Applications

**Child Participation**: The Four Questions are traditionally asked by the youngest child capable of asking them. This resource provides both Hebrew and English, allowing children of varying Hebrew proficiency to participate meaningfully. Children who can't yet read Hebrew can use the transliteration, while those learning Hebrew can follow along with the phonetic spelling.

**Sing-Along Format**: The layout with repetitions and line breaks mirrors the musical phrasing, making it easy for community Seders to distribute as a handout so everyone can join in singing, not just the child asking the questions.

**Bilingual Accessibility**: At community Seders where guests may have limited Hebrew knowledge, projecting or distributing this text ensures everyone understands what's being asked, transforming a ritualistic moment into an educational one.

**Teaching Opportunity**: After the child sings the questions, the Seder leader can briefly explain each answer (as outlined in the "Explained" section above) before proceeding with the full Haggadah narrative which provides the detailed responses.

**Translation Choices**: The translation "Why is this night so very different" adds emphasis ("so very") beyond the literal Hebrew, making the question more dramatic and engaging for English speakers, particularly children.

**Vegetable vs. Herbs Clarification**: The English version says "all kinds of vegetables" for all other nights and "bitter herbs" for this night, which is pedagogically clearer than saying "herbs" for both, helping participants understand why we specifically eat bitter herbs rather than sweet or neutral vegetables.

**Pre-Seder Preparation**: Shluchim can use this sheet for pre-Seder practice sessions with children, either in Sunday school/Hebrew school in the weeks before Pesach, or in a brief session right before the Seder begins. The phonetic Hebrew makes practice accessible even for children with limited Hebrew school background.

**Multiple Formats**: This can be:
  - Printed on cardstock for children to hold while singing
  - Projected on screens at large community Seders
  - Included in custom Haggadah booklets
  - Sent to families in advance for home practice
  - Used in model Seders or Pesach workshops

**Inclusive Participation**: At Seders where no young children are present, or where children are too shy to sing alone, this format allows the entire table to sing together, maintaining the participatory spirit even when the traditional "youngest child asks" model doesn't apply.

**Language Learning**: For families wanting to increase Hebrew literacy, this resource serves double duty - children learn the Hebrew sounds (through transliteration) while simultaneously learning the meanings (through translation), building vocabulary and comprehension.

**Cultural Authenticity**: Providing the Hebrew (even transliterated) maintains cultural authenticity and connection to Jewish tradition worldwide, while the English ensures comprehension and prevents the questions from becoming mere rote recitation without understanding.

**Melodic Preservation**: The structure explicitly notes "To the traditional tune of Mah Nishtana," signaling that this isn't meant to be read but sung, preserving the musical tradition that makes the Four Questions memorable and emotionally resonant across generations.

**Repetition as Pedagogy**: The repeated phrases ("but on this night, but on this night") aren't just for musical rhythm - they emphasize the central point: this night is different. The repetition helps participants, especially children, internalize this foundational concept.

**Adaptable for Abilities**: Shluchim can modify usage based on participants:
  - Very young children: English only, focus on one or two questions
  - Elementary age: Transliteration with English support
  - Hebrew school students: Challenge to read Hebrew directly (with this as backup)
  - Adult learners: Transliteration provides entry point to Hebrew participation

**Creating Anticipation**: The Four Questions formally launch the storytelling portion of the Seder. Having children prepare and successfully perform this creates pride and investment in the entire Seder experience, encouraging continued engagement throughout the evening.

**Resource Efficiency**: As a single-page document, this is easy to photocopy in bulk for large community Seders without significant expense, and can be reused year after year if printed on durable paper or laminated.

**Integration with Technology**: The simple text format makes it easy to integrate into digital presentations, Zoom Seders (screen share), or custom Seder apps, extending usability beyond print.
